\begin{abstract}
Optimizing a linear fractional function $\Phi$ over the efficient set of a
multi-objective integer program is usually done by repeatedly truncating the
\emph{decision} space with Sylva--Crema cuts. Each cut states a disjunction, so
it costs $p$ binary variables and $p+1$ big-$M$ rows, and the sub-problem
solved at every iteration is strictly larger than the one before it. We first
move the unexplored part of the problem into \emph{criterion} space, as a list
of boxes: the truncation becomes a box subtraction, every sub-problem is the
original feasible set plus a handful of ordinary linear rows, and the model
never grows. On 35 instances this is faster on 34, by $5.05\times$ overall and
$9.01\times$ on the heaviest. Re-measured on thirty instances per
configuration, the margin widens monotonically in $p$: median ratios of
$1.52\times$, $2.69\times$, $4.99\times$, $7.93\times$ and $14.57\times$ for
$p = 2 \dots 6$. By $p = 6$ the two methods solve \emph{the same number} of
sub-programs and one still takes $14.57\times$ as long, so the whole margin
lies in the cost of a single sub-problem --- which is the cost model above,
read off the measurement rather than argued.

We then hybridize that exact search three ways and report what each is worth.
Handing it the unfinished work of the published method (exact--exact)
\emph{ties} it. Seeding it from a Pareto local search with an archive
(exact--metaheuristic) is the one hybridization that pays, though less than a
first sample suggested: a median of $1.10\times$ over thirty paired instances,
with the seed exactly optimal on $24$ of them. Seeding it from the augmented
weighted Tchebychev program --- the scalarization the surrounding literature
generates efficient points with --- \textbf{never pays}: $1.02\times$ on the
same thirty, a wash, where a smaller sample had read $0.71\times$ and this note
called it a loss.

Two further changes sharpen the exact search without altering what it returns.
Pruning the open list the moment its best bound cannot beat the incumbent
removes a quarter of the sub-problems and no measurable time; stacked on the
metaheuristic seed it removes 4\% rather than 25\%, because the two feed the
same bound comparison and are substitutes rather than complements. And not
re-deriving a theorem of Ecker and Kouada once per inefficient box removes
$6\%$ of the branch \& bound nodes at an identical box count. Both are reported
in work rather than in seconds, because both effects are smaller than this
machine's noise floor and no number of repeats changes that.  A third, warm
starting each box from its parent's basis, removes $11\%$ of the simplex
pivots at an identical box count.

Two results arrived late and are the ones we would keep if only two could be
kept.  Completing the front to the whole efficient set was dominated by the
slice scan; \emph{solving} the $p$ slice equations instead of pruning on them,
which eliminates that many variables before the scan starts, is worth
$49\times$ in the median and up to $130\times$.  And a Pareto walk that hands
its verified archive to the front enumeration --- a seed that removes the cost
of \emph{discovering} a vector rather than the cost of proving one optimal ---
reaches the whole of $\E$ at $1.31\times$ the pure exact method over 150
instances, faster on 139 of them.  That hybridization pays where three earlier
ones did not, for a reason the earlier failures predict: the verification its
seeds need is a test the enumeration was going to pay anyway.  It also had to
be ordered the way the search's own probe would meet the points; in archive
order the same hybrid \emph{loses}.
Against those, four separate changes that remove a fifth to a quarter of the
sub-problems buy nothing at all, and we say why rather than leave a fifth
attempt to someone: in this search the sub-problems that can be removed
cheaply are the ones that were already cheap.
The Tchebychev result is the informative one, and it does not say the
scalarization is weak. As a generator it is strictly stronger than a weighted sum, reaching
unsupported efficient points no positive weighted sum can reach. It is a poor
\emph{seed} because it steers in criterion space relative to the ideal point
and never looks at $\Phi$. Symmetrically, the same free optimum that saves the
box search a third of its work saves the decision-space method \emph{zero}
iterations. What a heuristic incumbent is worth is a property of the exact
method it is attached to, not of the heuristic; we measure this in both
directions. All arithmetic is exact and rational, every answer is proved
optimal and cross-checked against independent references, and the negative
results and two corrected measurement errors are reported alongside the wins.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem}

Let
\begin{equation}
  (P_{\Dset}) \qquad \text{``}\max\text{''} \quad
  \Z_k(x) = \frac{c_k^{\top}x + a_k}{d_k^{\top}x + b_k},
  \quad k = 1,\dots,p,
  \qquad x \in \Dset = \{\, x \in \Zint^n_+ : Ax \leqslant b \,\},
\end{equation}
with $\Dset$ non-empty and bounded and every denominator
$D_k(x) = d_k^{\top}x + b_k$ strictly positive on $\Dset$. A point
$x \in \Dset$ is \emph{efficient} when no $y \in \Dset$ satisfies
$\Z(y) \geqslant \Z(x)$ with at least one strict inequality; $\E$ denotes the
set of efficient points. Linear criteria are the degenerate case $d_k = 0$,
$b_k = 1$.

Given the decision maker's criterion
$\Phi(x) = (U^{\top}x + \alpha)/(V^{\top}x + \beta)$, the problem is
\begin{equation}
  (P_E) \qquad \max \ \Phi(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x \in \E .
\end{equation}
$\E$ is a union of faces of $\Dset$: non-convex, with no explicit description.
The point of any method for $(P_E)$ is to reach the global optimum
\emph{without enumerating} $\E$.

An efficient point is \emph{supported} when it maximizes some strictly positive
weighted sum $w^{\top}\Z$ over $\Dset$ --- equivalently, when its criterion
vector lies on the convex hull of the criterion image. The distinction matters
throughout Section~\ref{sec:tcheb}: in an integer program unsupported efficient
points are common, and no weighted sum reaches any of them.

\section{Where the cost of the decision-space method comes from}
\label{sec:cost}

One iteration of the published method maximizes $\Phi$ over a truncated region
$\Dset_l$, tests the maximizer $x_l$ for efficiency, banks the best $\Phi$ on
the criterion slice about to be destroyed, and then truncates:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cut}
  \text{delete} \quad \{\, x : \Z(x) \leqslant \Z(\tilde{x}) \,\} .
\end{equation}
Keeping the complement means keeping the $x$ for which \emph{some} criterion
strictly improves on $\tilde{x}$. That is a disjunction, and a disjunction is
not a system of linear inequalities. It is modelled with one binary per
criterion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bigM}
  e_k(x;\tilde{x}) \geqslant 1 \cdot y_k + M_k (1 - y_k), \quad k = 1,\dots,p,
  \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{p} y_k \geqslant 1 ,
\end{equation}
where $M_k$ is a lower bound on $e_k$ over the region, so $y_k = 0$ leaves its
row vacuous and $y_k = 1$ forces a strict improvement.

\textbf{Every cut therefore adds $p$ binaries and $p+1$ rows.} On the heaviest
instance we ship, eleven cuts leave $33$ binaries and $44$ rows on top of
$\Dset$, and the late sub-problems cost far more than the early ones. This is
the whole of the method's cost profile: the number of iterations is modest, but
each one is solved on a strictly larger mixed-integer program than the last.

This observation fixes the cost model used for every comparison in this note:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
  \text{total cost} \;\approx\; (\text{number of sub-problems solved})
  \times (\text{size of a sub-problem}).
\end{equation}
The two factors are attacked by different ideas. Section~\ref{sec:box} removes
the growth of the second. Sections~\ref{sec:meta} and~\ref{sec:tcheb} attack
the first, and the measurements there are only interpretable because the second
has already been made constant.

\section{The same truncation in criterion space}\label{sec:box}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cut} deletes a \emph{box} from $\R^p$. If the unexplored
part of the problem is carried as a list of boxes rather than as a region of
$\Zint^n$, the truncation is a rewrite of that list, and each sub-problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sub}
  \max \ \Phi(x) \qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad x \in \Dset, \quad \text{the box's rows},
\end{equation}
which is $\Dset$ plus at most $2p$ ordinary linear rows. No binary variable
appears, and \textbf{the model never grows}: a box deep in the search carries a
few more rows than a shallow one, and that is all.

\subsection{How a box is written, and why not with numbers}

The obvious encoding, $l_k \leqslant \Z_k(x) \leqslant u_k$, fails. In the
general case $\Z_k$ is a ratio, so its values are rational, and the exact
``$+1$'' step that the split below needs would be lost.

Write the box with the linear form of the paper's Theorem~4 instead:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:e}
  e_k(x;a) \;=\; D_k(a)\, D_k(x) \bigl( \Z_k(x) - \Z_k(a) \bigr)
  \;=\; \bigl(D_k(a) c_k - N_k(a) d_k\bigr)^{\top} x
      + \bigl(D_k(a) a_k - N_k(a) b_k\bigr).
\end{equation}
Both denominators being positive, $e_k$ carries the sign of
$\Z_k(x) - \Z_k(a)$ while being \emph{linear in $x$}; with integer data it is
integer-valued on integer points. Hence, exactly and with no minimal step to
estimate,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact}
  \Z_k(x) > \Z_k(a) \iff e_k(x;a) \geqslant 1,
  \qquad
  \Z_k(x) \leqslant \Z_k(a) \iff e_k(x;a) \leqslant 0 .
\end{equation}
Linear and fractional criteria therefore go through the same code, and on a
linear criterion $e_k$ collapses to $c_k^{\top}x - c_k^{\top}a$.

\subsection{The split}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:split}
Let $B$ be a box and $a \in \Dset$. For $k = 1,\dots,p$ put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:child}
  B_k \;=\; B \;\cap\; \{\, x : e_k(x;a) \geqslant 1 \,\}
            \;\cap\; \bigcap_{j<k} \{\, x : e_j(x;a) \leqslant 0 \,\} .
\end{equation}
Then $B_1,\dots,B_p$ are pairwise disjoint and
$\bigcup_k B_k = B \setminus \{\, x : \Z(x) \leqslant \Z(a) \,\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in B$ with $\Z(x) \not\leqslant \Z(a)$. The set of indices at which $x$
strictly beats $\Z(a)$ is non-empty; let $k$ be its smallest element. By
\eqref{eq:exact}, $e_k(x;a) \geqslant 1$, and $e_j(x;a) \leqslant 0$ for every
$j < k$, so $x \in B_k$. Conversely a point of $B_k$ has
$e_k \geqslant 1$, hence $\Z(x) \not\leqslant \Z(a)$. Disjointness: membership
of $B_k$ forces $k$ to be exactly that smallest index, and a point has only
one.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Each of the $p$ children is the parent's row list plus at most $k$ further
rows, all of them ordinary linear inequalities in $x$.

\subsection{Bounding, and why it is what makes seeding possible}
\label{sec:bound}

The sub-problem~\eqref{eq:sub} maximizes $\Phi$ over a \emph{superset} of the
efficient points whose criterion vector lies in the box, so its value bounds
all of them. A box whose bound does not beat the incumbent is therefore
discarded \emph{whole} and never split. Children inherit their parent's value
as a bound, which makes the open list a certified upper bound on everything
still unfound; the method is \emph{anytime} in the same sense, and for the same
reason, as the decision-space one.

This is the single structural fact that Sections~\ref{sec:meta}
and~\ref{sec:tcheb} rest on. A better incumbent does not merely stop the search
sooner; it deletes subtrees. The decision-space method has no such mechanism,
which is measured in Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry} and is the reason the two
methods respond so differently to the very same incumbent.

\subsection{Stopping when the best bound still open is hopeless}
\label{sec:exit}

The open list is kept in a heap ordered by inherited bound \emph{descending},
and only the root --- and the boxes a hybrid hands over --- carry no bound;
those rank first. So by the time a bounded box reaches the top, every box still
open is bounded too, and if this one cannot beat the incumbent, neither can any
of them. The search is finished, and the remaining list is busywork.

Measured before the change was made, on the 18 instances used throughout
Sections~\ref{sec:meta}--\ref{sec:tcheb}: 198 of 733 sub-problems (27\%) were
solved despite carrying an inherited bound the incumbent already beat --- and
on \emph{every single instance} that count equalled the number of boxes still
open when the condition first fired. The waste is exactly a tail, never
scattered, which is what the heap order predicts and which is why a single
early exit is the whole of the fix rather than a per-box filter. Stopping there
takes 733 boxes to 549, \textbf{25\% fewer}, with the same answers, all still
proved optimal.

\paragraph{It is not a speed-up, and we do not report it as one.} The total is
$7.44$\,s against $7.27$\,s at per-instance minima over five repeats, with
per-instance ratios scattered from $0.77\times$ to $1.36\times$. The change can
only \emph{remove} work, so every ratio below $1.00\times$ is machine noise ---
and the noise is larger than the effect.

That is a confirmation rather than a disappointment, and it sharpens the cost
model of \eqref{eq:cost}. The tail boxes are the \emph{cheapest} ones: each is
killed by the root relaxation the instant it is handed the cutoff, so a quarter
of the sub-problems were genuinely wasted and worth almost nothing. Where the
count of sub-problems and the cost of the search come apart, it is the count
that is the easier number to improve and the weaker one to quote.

\section{Two obstacles specific to a fractional objective}

Criterion-space search is a known family for optimizing a \emph{linear}
function over the efficient set. It does not transfer to $(P_E)$ by
inspection, for two reasons.

\begin{remark}[$\Phi$ is a function of $x$, not of $\Z(x)$]
\label{rem:notZ}
Two feasible points with the same criterion vector may have different values of
$\Phi$. Enumerating non-dominated vectors and evaluating $\Phi$ on a
representative of each is therefore wrong. Inside every box a genuine
fractional integer program must be solved, and before cutting on an efficient
centre $\tilde{x}$ the sub-problem
$Q(\tilde{x}) = \max \{\, \Phi(x) : x \in \Dset,\ \Z(x) = \Z(\tilde{x}) \,\}$
must be solved first, since the whole slice $\{\Z = \Z(\tilde{x})\}$ is
efficient and is about to be removed.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Box boundaries must be integer-valued]
As argued above, numeric bounds on $\Z_k$ are rational in the fractional case.
Writing the boundaries through $e_k$ restores an exact integral threshold and,
as a by-product, makes fractional criteria work with no special case.
\end{remark}

\section{The algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Criterion-space search for $(P_E)$, optionally seeded}
\label{alg:box}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $L \gets \{\, \R^p \,\}$
\State $(x_{\mathrm{opt}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}) \gets$ a \emph{verified
       efficient} seed and its value, or $(\varnothing, -\infty)$
\While{$L \neq \varnothing$}
  \State remove from $L$ a box $B$ of largest inherited bound
  \State solve \eqref{eq:sub} on $B$ with cutoff $\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$
  \If{infeasible, or its value $\leqslant \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$}
     \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{$B$ is discarded whole}
  \EndIf
  \State let $x_B$ be its maximizer
  \If{$x_B$ is efficient}
     \State $\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}} \gets \Phi(x_B)$; \
            $x_{\mathrm{opt}} \gets x_B$; \ $a \gets x_B$
     \Statex \Comment{$x_B$ already maximizes $\Phi$ on its whole slice, so no $Q$ is owed}
  \Else
     \State $a \gets$ an efficient point dominating $x_B$
     \State solve $Q(a)$ and update $(\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}, x_{\mathrm{opt}})$
  \EndIf
  \State add to $L$ the $p$ children of $B$ given by \eqref{eq:child}
\EndWhile
\State \Return $(x_{\mathrm{opt}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}})$, proved optimal
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Termination: every pass removes at least the criterion vector $\Z(a)$, and the
non-dominated set of a bounded integer program is finite.

Line 2 is the whole of the hybridization interface. Everything in
Sections~\ref{sec:meta} and~\ref{sec:tcheb} is a different way of filling it,
and the correctness requirement is stated once, in Proposition~\ref{prop:seed}
below: the seed must be \emph{efficient}, not merely good.

\begin{proposition}[Soundness of an arbitrary seed]\label{prop:seed}
If the seed $x_0$ in line 2 is efficient, Algorithm~\ref{alg:box} returns the
same $(x_{\mathrm{opt}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}})$ as the unseeded run, and
$\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is the global optimum of $(P_E)$. If $x_0$ is merely
feasible, the algorithm may return a strictly suboptimal value.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is used only as a cutoff, and a box is discarded when its
bound fails to \emph{beat} it. If $x_0 \in \E$ then
$\Phi(x_0) \leqslant \max_{x \in \E}\Phi(x)$, so no box containing an optimal
criterion vector is ever discarded, and the incumbent is a feasible value of
$(P_E)$ throughout; the two runs therefore agree. If $x_0 \notin \E$ then
$\Phi(x_0)$ may exceed the optimum of $(P_E)$ --- a dominated point can score
arbitrarily well on $\Phi$ --- and the box containing the true optimum is
discarded by the very first bound comparison.
\end{proof}

\noindent
This is why every seed in this note is \emph{verified} before it is used, and
why that verification, not the heuristic, turns out to be the cost of
Section~\ref{sec:meta}.

\subsection{Not paying twice for Ecker \& Kouada}
\label{sec:ecker}

When a box's maximiser $x_B$ fails the efficiency test, the algorithm needs an
efficient point to cut on in its place, and the obvious way to get one is to
walk the dominance chain from the test's witness until a test returns
$\theta = 0$. With \emph{linear} criteria that walk is over before it starts,
and the program that discovers this can be skipped.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ecker}
With linear criteria, every optimal solution of the efficiency test applied to
$x^{*}$ is efficient.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $d_k = 0$ the test maximizes
$\sum_k e_k(y; x^{*}) = \sum_k \bigl(\Z_k(y) - \Z_k(x^{*})\bigr)$, a strictly
positive weighted sum of $\Z$ up to an additive constant, over
$R = \{\, y \in \Dset : \Z(y) \geqslant \Z(x^{*}) \,\}$. Let $y^{*}$ be a
maximizer and suppose $z \in \Dset$ dominated it. Then
$\Z(z) \geqslant \Z(y^{*}) \geqslant \Z(x^{*})$, so $z \in R$; and
$\sum_k \Z_k(z) > \sum_k \Z_k(y^{*})$ because at least one component is
strictly larger. That contradicts optimality, so no maximizer is dominated ---
ties included, since the argument never assumes $y^{*}$ unique.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The proposition is due to Ecker and Kouada; what is measured here is the cost
of not using it. With \emph{fractional} criteria it is false, and the proof
says where: the $k$-th term of the objective carries a factor $D_k(y)$ that
varies from point to point, the objective stops being monotone in $\Z$, and a
dominating point can score lower than a dominated one. There the walk is paid.

Checked before being relied on: 349 witnesses of failed tests across 60
linear-criteria instances, none of them dominated.

\subsection{Paying the efficiency test less often}
\label{sec:known}

Splitting the work by operation puts the cost somewhere the wall-clock profile
did not: the efficiency test is \textbf{52\% of all simplex pivots on 146
calls}, 58 pivots each against $11.8$ for a box sub-problem. The useful
question is therefore not how to make the test cheaper but how to pay it less
often.

When a box's maximiser $x_B$ is inefficient, the test is what produces the
efficient point to cut on. But the search already holds the points it has
\emph{proved} efficient, and asking whether one of them dominates $x_B$ is
arithmetic rather than an integer program. One does on 21 of 146 tests
(14\%), and on 17\% of the inefficient cases.

\noindent
The box count \emph{falling} was not the prediction. Among the remembered
points dominating $x_B$ the highest is taken, and \eqref{eq:child} removes more
of the box the higher its centre, so skipping the test and cutting better
compound instead of trading off.

\paragraph{The invariant this rests on.} Every point the search remembers is
efficient: it remembers the centres it cut on and, beside each, the maximiser
of $Q$, which shares its centre's criterion vector and is therefore efficient
whenever the centre is. One inefficient member would let the search cut on a
dominated point and remove the true optimum, so this is pinned by a test rather
than by an argument. Unlike the two changes above, this one deliberately
\emph{changes} which boxes are produced, so it is checked against the answer
and the proof rather than against a box count.

\subsection{Warm-starting a box from its parent}
\label{sec:warm}

A box's region is its parent's plus a handful of rows, yet each box solved its
root relaxation from scratch. A simplex phase~I is needed whenever a row is not
covered by a slack column, which every $e_k \geqslant 1$ row of
\eqref{eq:child} forces. Measured before anything was built: \textbf{4992 of
18193 simplex pivots, 27\%, were phase~I inside cold roots}, and 788 of 824
roots paid one --- $6.3$ pivots each against $2.3$ for phase~II.

The parent's basis stays a basis of the child once the new rows' slacks join
it, so only those rows can be primal infeasible and a dual restoration repairs
them. That is the argument branch \& bound already uses for its own nodes,
applied one level up; all it needs is the general form of the single-variable
row that branching adds.

\paragraph{The ceiling was never 27\%.} Only a box with a parent can inherit
one, and that is 63\% of the roots. The rest are the efficiency tests and the
$Q$ sub-problems, which extend $\Dset$ rather than another box --- and caching
a basis for $\Dset$ would buy nothing, because its rows are all $\leqslant$
with non-negative right-hand sides, so its crash basis is free and phase~I is
skipped outright. The reachable ceiling was about $17\%$, of which $11.1\%$
survives the restoration's own $1583$ pivots. The gain grows with $p$ ($28\%$
at $p = 7$ against $1$--$7\%$ at $p = 3$), which is what more rows per box
predicts, and two very small instances lose a little where the restoration
costs more than the phase~I it replaces.

\paragraph{517 warm starts, 0 stalls.} The fallback to a cold solve never
fired. It is kept regardless, because a restoration that gives up must cost
time and never correctness --- which is also why the box counts above are
identical rather than merely equal in the answers they produce.

\section{First hybridization: exact--exact}

Since the published method closes in one iteration whenever the maximizer of
$\Phi$ over $\Dset$ happens to be efficient, a hybrid suggests itself: run its
loop, and if it has not closed after $s$ iterations, hand the unfinished work
to the box search. The handover is not a restart. Replaying each cut as a box
subtraction (Proposition~\ref{prop:split} applied to every box of the current
list) reproduces the region the first phase reached, and the incumbent, being
attained at a known efficient point, is a valid seed by
Proposition~\ref{prop:seed}.

Measured on 24 instances against the better of the two pure methods on each:

\section{Second hybridization: exact--metaheuristic}\label{sec:meta}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:seed} the box search will accept any efficient point
as a seed, and by Section~\ref{sec:bound} a good one deletes subtrees. That
makes a metaheuristic the natural first phase --- it is cheap and it is guided
by $\Phi$, which is exactly the quantity the cutoff compares against.

\subsection{The heuristic}

A Pareto local search maintains an archive of mutually non-dominated points.
From several random maximal starting points it walks the neighbourhood of each
archive member, admitting any neighbour the archive does not dominate. The
archive \emph{is} the search: restarting from fresh random points between walks
throws it away, and four times the effort did not compensate for that
(obtaining $0.360$, $0.280$ and $0.458$ of the optimum on three instances where
the archive-driven version obtained the optimum).

The neighbourhood needs \emph{swap} moves ($-1$ on one coordinate, $+1$ on
another), not only unit steps. From a maximal point a unit step up is
infeasible, and a unit step down lowers every criterion with non-negative
coefficients, so the archive refuses it and the walk dies immediately: three
archive points and an incumbent at 13\% of the optimum. With swaps the archive
holds 28--69 points and the incumbent reaches 60--100\%.

\subsection{Where correctness lives}

The archive is filtered by dominance \emph{among the points it has seen}, which
is not the exact efficiency test. Proposition~\ref{prop:seed} makes that gap
fatal if ignored, so nothing crosses unverified: the best archive member is put
through the exact test, and if it fails, it is walked to an efficient point
dominating it and that point's value is used instead. The heuristic decides
\emph{where to look}; it never decides what is true.

The gap is not hypothetical. Over a wide sweep, one archive member in roughly
2000 turned out not to be efficient --- reproducibly, a concrete case being a
four-variable instance whose archive retains $(2,1,1,1)$. Rare is not never.

\subsection{What it costs, and what it is worth}

The cost is \emph{not} the walk, which takes $0.01$--$0.08$\,s and is barely
sensitive to its budget. We assumed the opposite and optimized the walk first;
the timings then showed the total \emph{falling as the walk's budget rose},
because a poor archive yields candidates needing longer repair chains, each one
an integer program. The cost is the verification --- the price of
Proposition~\ref{prop:seed}. Examining the single best archive member gave
exactly the same incumbent as examining four on every instance tried, at a
quarter to a half of the cost.

\section{The asymmetry: a seed is worth what the exact method can do with it}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

The ceiling experiment can be run on the published method too, and this is the
comparison we consider the most transferable result in this note.

  & none \\

\end{tabular}

\noindent
The reason is structural. What the decision-space method cuts at iteration $l$
is decided by the efficiency test applied to $x_l$, through
\eqref{eq:cut}; $\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$ enters the sub-problem only as a cutoff
inside a branch-and-bound that would have found the same $x_l$ anyway. A box,
by contrast, is compared against the incumbent \emph{before} it is ever split,
and failing that comparison deletes it together with every descendant it would
have had.

So ``hybridize an exact method with a metaheuristic'' is not one question with
one answer. The heuristic is identical in both rows above; what differs is
whether the exact method has a mechanism through which a lower bound can remove
work. Before building such a hybrid, the mechanism is what should be checked.

\subsection{Two ideas that feed the same mechanism do not add}
\label{sec:substitutes}

The same reasoning has a consequence that is easy to miss when improvements are
collected one at a time. The early exit of Section~\ref{sec:exit} and the
metaheuristic seed of Section~\ref{sec:meta} look independent --- one prunes at
the top of the list, the other starts the incumbent higher --- but they act
through the \emph{same} mechanism, the bound comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:bound}. So they should not be expected to compose, and
measured, they do not:

with both.}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}

We record this because the arithmetic of stacked improvements is where
plausible reasoning goes wrong most cheaply. Two ideas add only when they feed
different mechanisms; two that feed the same one are one idea, measured twice.

\section{Third hybridization: the Tchebychev reference}\label{sec:tcheb}

The surrounding literature generates efficient points with the augmented
weighted Tchebychev program, so a fair evaluation of the two hybrids above has
to be made against it and not only against a weighted sum. We therefore
implement it and measure it, rather than cite it.

\subsection{The program}

With $z^{*}$ the ideal point, $z^{*}_k = \max\{\Z_k(x) : x \in \Dset\}$,
weights $w > 0$ and an augmentation $\rho > 0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tcheb}
  \min_{x \in \Dset} \quad \max_{k} \; w_k\bigl(z^{*}_k - \Z_k(x)\bigr)
  \;+\; \rho \sum_{k=1}^{p} \bigl(z^{*}_k - \Z_k(x)\bigr),
\end{equation}
which linearizes with a single continuous variable $\lambda$ subject to
$\lambda \geqslant w_k (z^{*}_k - \Z_k(x))$ for each $k$. Each weight vector
therefore costs one integer program on a model with $n+1$ variables and $p$
extra rows, plus $p$ programs paid once for $z^{*}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tcheb}
For any $w > 0$ and any $\rho > 0$, every optimal solution of
\eqref{eq:tcheb} is efficient.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x^{*}$ be optimal and suppose some $y \in \Dset$ dominated it, so
$\Z(y) \geqslant \Z(x^{*})$ with at least one strict inequality. Then
$z^{*}_k - \Z_k(y) \leqslant z^{*}_k - \Z_k(x^{*})$ for every $k$, so the
$\max$ term does not increase; and since $\rho > 0$ and at least one
inequality is strict, the sum term \emph{strictly} decreases. The objective at
$y$ would be strictly smaller than at $x^{*}$, contradicting optimality.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The augmentation is exactly what makes the second half of that argument
available: with $\rho = 0$ the objective at $y$ need only be no larger, and a
merely \emph{weakly} efficient point can minimize the program. Our
implementation therefore refuses $\rho \leqslant 0$ and refuses a zero weight
rather than returning a point whose status is in doubt. It also refuses
fractional criteria, for which $z^{*}_k - \Z_k(x)$ is not linear and the rows
above are not constraints of an integer linear program; the references
assume linear criteria as well.

\subsection{As a generator it is strictly stronger than a weighted sum}

A weighted sum maximizes only at supported efficient points. Varying $w$ in
\eqref{eq:tcheb} reaches unsupported ones too, and that is what the literature
uses it for.

\centering

\midrule

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

All 540 optima returned were efficient, as Proposition~\ref{prop:tcheb}

\paragraph{Cost.} It is $p+1$ integer programs on an enlarged model. It is

\section{Delivering a subset of the efficient set}
\label{sec:subset}

Everything above answers one question --- which efficient point maximises
$\Phi$ --- and proves the answer.  The work this note implements states a
second one as its expected result: \emph{a subset of efficient solutions,
generated by hybrid algorithms}.  That subset is what a decision maker looks
at, and the machinery could already produce it without ever exporting it.

Two sources exist, and a third was measured and rejected.

\paragraph{Certification is the cost.}  The archive filters by dominance among
the points it has \emph{seen}, which is not the exact efficiency test; about

instances small enough to enumerate; where $\E$ cannot be enumerated, what
fraction was found is unknowable, and the implementation says so rather than
reporting a percentage it cannot support.  The exact search's trail is also a
\emph{biased} sample --- it is precisely the points the search had to cut on, so
it concentrates where $\Phi$ is large.  For a decision maker ranking by $\Phi$
that bias points the useful way, but it is a bias and is reported as one.

\section{The complete front, in criterion space}
\label{sec:front}

Section~\ref{sec:subset} delivers a subset and refuses to claim completeness.
That refusal is honest but it is also a limitation, and the same substitution
this note is built on removes it.

Complete enumeration of the non-dominated set was already available, but in
\emph{decision} space: truncate $\Dset$ with Sylva--Crema cuts, pick a point,
repair it, cut again.  Every cut costs $p$ binaries and $p+1$ big-$M$ rows, so
the sub-problems grow --- exactly the cost profile Section~\ref{sec:box}
replaces.  Keeping the unexplored region as a list of boxes instead gives the
same enumeration with no binary anywhere and no model growth.

this.}  Counting branch \& bound nodes, criterion space explores \emph{more} of
them --- the ratio is $0.29\times$, so about three and a half times as many ---
and still finishes four times sooner.  Its nodes carry no binary and no
big-$M$ row, so each is far cheaper: it wins by solving many easy programs
instead of few hard ones.  The same count in Table~\ref{tab:margin} goes the
other way at every $p$, because there the two methods solve nearly the same
number of sub-programs and the decision-space ones are the harder.  Both
readings say the cost lives in the size of a sub-problem, which is exactly what
the substitution removes.

\subsection{Is the baseline fair?  Where the decision-space time actually goes}
\label{sec:probe}

A $204\times$ margin invites the suspicion that the loser was handicapped, so
we profiled it rather than quoting it.  On the $n=5$ instance, of $137$\,s
spent producing 30 vectors, \textbf{$137.21$\,s --- 100\% --- is inside the
probe}, over 31 calls and 14983 branch \& bound nodes; the repair takes
$0.09$\,s and adding the cuts $0.02$\,s.  The model grows from 5 columns and 7
rows to 95 and 127, which is exactly $p$ binaries and $p+1$ rows per cut.  The
cost is therefore structural --- it is the growth of the sub-problems, which is
the thing Section~\ref{sec:box} removes --- and not an artefact of some
inefficiency on the side of the comparison.

One candidate handicap remained.  The probe only needs \emph{some} feasible
point of the truncated region, yet it maximises a linear direction; stopping at
the first integer point found looks strictly cheaper.  Measured, it is the
opposite:

\noindent
The \textbf{cut counts are identical}: the direction changes neither how many
vectors there are nor the order they come out in.  What explodes is the branch
\& bound inside each probe, by a factor of two to six, because a zero objective
gives it nothing to prune with --- every node's relaxation is worth $0$, so no
bound can discard a subtree until an integer point has been stumbled upon.  The
direction is load-bearing rather than decorative, the criterion-space method
uses the same one, and the $204\times$ stands.

We report this because it is a plausible-looking optimisation that makes things
four to thirteen times worse, and because it was checked while suspecting the
\emph{comparison} was unfair --- it was not.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:front}
The enumeration terminates, and records every non-dominated vector exactly
once.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Termination.}  The probe returns a point $x$ of $\Dset$ inside the box,
and the efficient point $a$ it is repaired to dominates it, so
$\Z(x) \leqslant \Z(a)$ and the split around $a$ removes at least $\Z(x)$ from
that box.  The unexplored region therefore shrinks on every pass, and the
non-dominated set of a bounded integer program is finite.

\emph{Completeness.}  A non-dominated vector $v$ leaves the region only through
a split around some centre $a$ with $v \leqslant \Z(a)$.  If $v \neq \Z(a)$,
that says $a$ dominates $v$, which no non-dominated vector admits.  So $v$
leaves only at the moment it is recorded; and the loop ends only when the
region is empty, so every $v$ is recorded, exactly once since the centres are
de-duplicated by criterion vector.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Completeness is a flag, not a default.}  It is set only when the box
list actually emptied.  A run stopped by a box budget or a time budget returns
what it has with the flag \emph{false} --- a front that cannot say it is whole
is a subset with extra steps, and saying so is the difference between the two
sections.

\paragraph{The front is not the set of efficient points.}  One non-dominated
vector can be attained by several efficient points, and the enumeration keeps
\emph{one per vector}: the front is complete while the set of points it carries
need not be.  On random instances the distinction never appeared --- over 32 of
them, 476 efficient points sat on 476 distinct vectors --- but that is a
property of the sample, since generic criterion coefficients make ties
improbable.  Constructed deliberately, with a criterion that ignores a
variable, $|\E| = 6$ sits on 2 vectors and the enumeration returns 2 points of
the 6.

That instance also shows what $Q$ is for.  Its slice $\Z = (1,2)$ holds three
efficient points with $\Phi = 3/4$, $6/5$ and $3/2$; the repair lands on the
first, and $Q$ moves it to the third.  So the largest value on the front is
still the optimum of $(P_E)$ --- and without $Q$ the answer would have been
\emph{half} of it, with the front itself still correct and the output looking
entirely reasonable.

\paragraph{Vectors are not solutions.}  By Remark~\ref{rem:notZ}, $\Phi$ is a
function of $x$ and not of $\Z(x)$, so one non-dominated vector can carry
several efficient points with different $\Phi$: enumerating the front does not
by itself answer $(P_E)$.  Each vector is therefore paired with the maximiser of
$\Phi$ \emph{on its slice}, at one extra integer program per vector --- the
pairing a decision maker wants, and the one a plain vector enumeration cannot
provide.

That pairing has a consequence worth stating, because it closes the circle of
this note: \textbf{the largest value on the front is the optimum of $(P_E)$.}
The front enumeration is therefore a second, independent exact method for the
problem, and the test suite uses it as a cross-check on the first.

\subsection{What the pruning is worth}

Two exact routes to $(P_E)$ now exist: \emph{prune} --- stop as soon as no open
box can beat the incumbent --- or \emph{enumerate} the whole front and take the
best value on it.  They share the box machinery but not the reason they stop,
so the comparison measures the pruning itself.

\section{From the front to the complete efficient set}
\label{sec:complete}

Section~\ref{sec:front} proves its vector list complete and says, in the same
breath, that the \emph{point} list is not: one vector is kept per slice.  That
was the last honest gap in the chain, and closing it turns out to cost almost
nothing, for a reason worth stating on its own.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:slice}
If $v$ is non-dominated, then \emph{every} $x \in \Dset$ with $\Z(x) = v$ is
efficient.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose some $y \in \Dset$ dominated such an $x$.  Then
$\Z(y) \geqslant \Z(x) = v$ with at least one strict inequality, which says $v$
is dominated --- against the hypothesis.  So no $y$ dominates $x$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Two consequences, and together they are the whole method.  First,
\[
  \E \;=\; \bigcup_{v \text{ non-dominated}}
                \{\, x \in \Dset : \Z(x) = v \,\},
\]
so the complete front plus its slices \emph{is} the complete efficient set.
Second, \textbf{not one efficiency test is paid on any slice point}: they are
efficient by Proposition~\ref{prop:slice}, not by examination.  The expensive
operation of this whole note --- the efficiency test of Section~\ref{sec:ecker} --- is
simply not invoked.

\paragraph{A slice is $\Dset$ plus $p$ equalities, and nothing more.}  Fixing
$\Z(x) = v$ is linear in $x$ \emph{even for fractional criteria}: since
$D_k(x) = d_k^{\top}x + b_k > 0$ on $\Dset$, the equation
$(c_k^{\top}x + a_k)/(d_k^{\top}x + b_k) = v_k$ clears to
\[
  (c_k - v_k d_k)^{\top} x \;=\; v_k b_k - a_k ,
\]
with no sign condition to check and no case split.  So a slice carries no
binary, no big-$M$, and nothing that grows from one slice to the next --- the
same property the box search has, arrived at a second time.  The equalities are
in fact the \emph{tightest} rows in the system, which is what makes the
depth-first scan over them cheap.

\paragraph{What it recovers, and what it costs.}  The family below fixes five
variables that enter $\Z$ and adds $f$ further variables that enter only their
own bound $0 \leqslant x_j \leqslant 4$.  Those are invisible to $\Z$, so every
slice is a grid of $5^{f}$ points and the front keeps one of them.

\noindent
At $f = 3$ the front carries $0.8\%$ of the efficient set --- it is complete
and it is missing $99.2\%$ of the points, which is exactly the distinction
Section~\ref{sec:front} insisted on --- and recovering all $1625$ of them
\textbf{never doubles the cost}.  The scan is output-sensitive: $0.167$\,s for
$1625$ points is about $0.1$\,ms each, against the $7.68$\,ms an efficiency
test costs, and there are no tests to pay.

\paragraph{The honest reading, which is conditional.}  At $f = 0$ the front
\emph{already was} the efficient set, and the $10$--$24\%$ buys only the proof
of that.  On generic random instances this is the usual case: over $32$ of
them, $476$ efficient points sat on $476$ distinct vectors.  So the value of
this section is not a speed-up and is not uniform.  It is that the method now
\emph{knows} which case it is in, instead of returning a point list whose
completeness depended on an assumption about the coefficients.  Where ties do
occur --- criteria that ignore a variable, repeated structure, any instance
that is not generic --- the front alone silently returns a fraction of the
answer, and the fraction can be under one percent.

\paragraph{Thirty instances of the generic family, measured.}  The campaign
confirms both halves of that reading on the family used everywhere else in this
note ($n=5$, $p=3$).  The overhead of completing the front has a median of
$24.6\%$, an interquartile range of $[21.2\%,\ 36.0\%]$ and a maximum of
$73.7\%$ --- never a doubling, on any of the thirty.  And \textbf{exactly one
of the thirty had a non-singleton slice}.  So on a generic instance the front
really is the efficient set almost always, and the complete enumeration almost
always buys only the proof of that.  The one instance in thirty is why the
proof is worth buying: nothing about the front's own output says which of the
two cases you are in.

\subsection{Solving the slice equations instead of pruning on them}
\label{sec:elim}

The scan above keeps all $n$ variables and feeds the $p$ slice equations to a
depth-first search as ordinary pruning rows.  That is wasteful, and the waste
is the dominant cost as soon as $n$ grows: profiling the complete enumeration
at $n \geqslant 9$ put $25$--$30$\,s in the slice scan against under a second
in the front enumeration, and at $n = 12$ a single slice could not be finished
inside a two-minute budget.

The equations are \emph{linear}.  So solve them.  Gaussian elimination in exact
rationals expresses $r$ of the variables as linear functions of the rest;
substituting them into every row of the model leaves an equivalent system in
$n - r$ unknowns, and the scan runs on that.  Two rows per eliminated variable
keep it inside its own box, so its bounds still prune.  At each leaf the
eliminated variables are rebuilt, and the one condition that is \emph{not}
linear --- that they come out integer --- is checked there.  An inconsistent
system settles the slice as empty without any scan at all.

The search space $(ub+1)^{n}$ becomes $(ub+1)^{n-r}$.

\midrule

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
Exactness is untouched: every coefficient is a rational and the elimination is
exact, so this changes the order of the search and nothing else.  It is also
the \emph{only} change in this note that ever beat the wall of
Section~\ref{sec:wall}, and it did so by attacking an exponent rather than a
count.

\paragraph{A sign error that ran, and returned nothing.}  The first draft had a
plus where the eliminated variables' contribution must be subtracted.  It ran
without error, looked like a working optimisation, and returned the
\emph{empty} set on 179 of 185 slices --- it would have dropped efficient
points from $\E$ silently.  Only the comparison against the un-eliminated scan
caught it, which is what that comparison is for, and the test suite keeps it.

\paragraph{What is verified.}  The complete set is checked against exhaustive
enumeration on two families, one generic and one built to force ties
($24$ instances, exact match, $21$ non-singleton slices).
Proposition~\ref{prop:slice} is checked directly rather than assumed: $74$
points on non-dominated slices, every one efficient.  The front's own
representative is checked to lie inside its slice.  And, as everywhere else in
this note, an exhausted budget returns \texttt{complete = False} rather than a
set that claims what it did not prove.

\section{Seeding the enumeration from a generator, which does not pay}
\label{sec:gen}

Section~\ref{sec:tcheb} found the augmented Tchebychev program a strong
\emph{generator} and a poor \emph{seed}, and blamed the second on its never
looking at $\Phi$.  Enumerating the front has no $\Phi$ to look at: the target
is the vectors themselves, so the reason it failed there does not apply.  It is
also the natural candidate, because its optima are efficient \emph{by
construction} --- no verification needed, which is what the metaheuristic seed
pays for.

The arithmetic to check, stated before measuring: seeding $k$ vectors removes
$k$ probes and $k$ efficiency tests, and costs $p$ programs for the ideal point
plus one per weight vector.

Every ratio is the wrong way, and the decisive row is the second: the wide grid

does.

The reason is structural.  Seeding $k$ distinct vectors saves at most $k$
probes and costs at least $k$ scalarisations, plus $p$ for the ideal point,
plus one for every weight that lands on a vector already seen.  And a
scalarisation is not cheaper than a probe:

and usually needs no branching at all; the scalarisation minimises a

branches.  It is cheaper than a probe on only 7 of 40 instances.  Even
crediting the seed with the $7.68$\,ms efficiency test it also removes ---
$17.2$\,ms saved against $20.9$\,ms spent --- a single seed is already a losing
trade before the ideal point and the duplicate weights are counted.

\paragraph{And the saving is not guaranteed either.}  Seeding was expected to
remove exactly one probe per seed, and in the median it does: $+1.00$ over 152
instances.  But the pre-split imposes a box structure the search would not have
chosen, and on \textbf{5 of those 152} the seeded run spent \emph{more} probes,
up to four more.  That observation is what Section~\ref{sec:order} turns into a
fix.

\section{The hybridization that pays: a walk that seeds the enumeration}
\label{sec:hybridfront}

Until this section nothing in this note was both \emph{hybrid} and
\emph{complete}.  $\E$ was reached by a purely exact method; every
hybridization had been attached to the optimisation search, and the one
attached to the enumeration --- a Tchebychev generator, Section~\ref{sec:gen}
--- lost.  That gap was real, and this closes it.

\subsection{Why a seed is worth something here at all}

A seed for the optimisation search feeds one mechanism: the \emph{bound}.  A
search that already knows a good value prunes boxes whose bound cannot beat it.
The front enumeration \textbf{has no bound and no incumbent} --- it must
exhaust every box --- so a seed of that kind is worth exactly nothing.

What it does have is a different mechanism.  Every box costs one probe, one
efficiency test and sometimes a repair, all spent to find \emph{a vector}; a
vector already known needs none of them.  Seeding here is not ``start from a
better value'' but ``do not pay to discover what you already know''.
Soundness is unchanged, because a seed enters the box list exactly where a
found point would have: the split around it is the same split, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:front} applies verbatim.

\subsection{Why the archive pays where the scalarisation did not}

Two things separate them, and both are measured.

\paragraph{Cost per vector.}  A Tchebychev scalarisation costs $20.90$\,ms and
buys one point; a Pareto walk costs about $0.011$\,s and buys a whole archive,
covering \textbf{95\% of the front} in the median.

\paragraph{The verification is free here, and only here.}  The walk proves
nothing, so every archive member has to be put through the exact efficiency
test --- and that verification was the cost that sank the metaheuristic seed
in Section~\ref{sec:meta}.  Here it is not a cost at all, because \textbf{the
enumeration was going to pay the same test anyway}: every probe the seed
removes was going to be followed by one.  The verification cancels and the
probe is the gain.  The Tchebychev generator has the opposite profile --- its
points need no verification, and it has no cheap way to produce them.

\subsection{The order is the whole difference}
\label{sec:order}

Handed over in archive order the hybrid \emph{lost}, at $0.81\times$, and the
probe count went \emph{up} on 4 of 12 instances: the pre-split was building a
box structure the search would not have chosen.  The probe maximises a fixed
linear direction, so the search splits first around whatever scores highest on
it.  Sorting the seeds by that same direction reproduces that structure
instead of fighting it.

\noindent
The probe column is monotone and certain: $32\%$ removed at $p=3$, $13\%$ at
$p=6$.  \textbf{It does not bound the time.}  A seed removes a probe on a
\emph{non-empty} box, which carries an efficiency test behind it, while the
probes left behind are mostly on empty boxes and are refuted at once --- so the
work removed is dearer than the average probe.  Measured,
$1/(\text{probes kept})$ and the time ratio agree only loosely and at $p=6$ the
time ratio exceeds it.  An earlier draft of this note turned the probe bound
into ``a hard ceiling of roughly $1.4\times$''; that is withdrawn.

What does grow with $|F|$ is the walk's cost, one efficiency test per archive
member.  A large front buys no more and costs more --- the ordering in the
table, and the same mechanism as Section~\ref{sec:cover} from the other side.

\subsection{Where the hybrid fails, and the line that fixes it}
\label{sec:cover}

At $n = 12$ the margin falls to $1.00\times$, and at $n=12$, $p=5$ to
$0.97\times$.  We first blamed the probe count outgrowing $|F|$.  \textbf{The
measurement refutes that}: probes per vector are flat across the range ---
$2.4$, $2.7$, $2.7$, $2.8$, $2.8$, $3.0$, $3.9$, $5.2$.  What collapses is the
share of the front the walk finds, and the speed-up tracks it line for line:
$87\%$ at $n=6$, $79\%$ at $n=9$, $65\%$ at $n=10$, $35\%$ at $n=12$, $12\%$ at
$n=12$, $p=5$.  A fixed budget of 8 restarts and 4000 neighbours is a fixed
amount of work against a target that grows --- and $4000$ was chosen at
$n=7,8$ and generalised.

Widening the walk alone confirms it and names the knob: at $n=12$, going from
8 to 16 restarts takes coverage from $35\%$ to $95\%$ and the ratio from
$1.03\times$ to $1.61\times$, while the walk's own time barely moves.  So the
first round is sized to the instance, at $\lceil n/4 \rceil$ times the
restarts.  Over 120 instances spanning $n = 6 \dots 12$ that gives a median
$\mathbf{1.36\times}$, faster on $116$.

\paragraph{But coverage is not the objective, and that bounds the idea.}  On
the same $p = 5$ instance, \textbf{$46\%$ coverage gives $1.20\times$ and
$93\%$ gives $1.12\times$}: buying the last half of the front costs more
walking than the probes it removes.  A walk that restarts in doubling rounds
until one finds nothing new takes coverage there from $45\%$ to $97\%$ and is
\emph{slower} --- $1.02\times$ against $1.14\times$, and a median
$1.32\times$ against $1.36\times$ over the 120, winning 109 against 116.  It
is kept in the code and switched off.

\paragraph{A correction to our own reading.}  The collapse was first reported
here as ``$35\%$ coverage, $1.00\times$'' from \emph{one} instance; over six
seeds of that configuration the unmodified walk averages $78\%$ and
$1.35\times$.  Severe where $|F|$ is large --- $45\%$ at $|F| = 302$ --- but
not as general as one instance made it look.  That is the fifth time in this
note that a single instance misled, which is why the campaign exists.

\section{A wall met four times, and the one change that went round it}
\label{sec:wall}

Four separate changes in this note remove between a fifth and a quarter of the
sub-problems and buy nothing.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Four attempts on the sub-problem count.}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
change & sub-problems removed & cost \\
\midrule
early exit on a hopeless bound      & $25\%$        & no measurable time \\
free contradiction filter           & $24.8\%$      & $0.98\times$ pivots \\
range filter (ideal + anti-ideal)   & $+11.3\%$     & $1.03\times$ pivots \\
dominated-whole filter              & $8$--$23\%$   & $1.077\times$ pivots \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
Only the second is kept, and it is worth $2\%$.  The reason is the same every
time, and after four it is a property rather than an accident:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{In this search, removing sub-problems does not remove time, because
the ones that can be removed cheaply are the ones that were already cheap.}
\end{quote}

An empty box --- and $73\%$ of them are empty --- is refuted by the simplex
almost at once: every probe in the families measured here costs \emph{zero}
branch \& bound nodes, because the relaxation is integral or infeasible at the
root.  A filter that catches empty boxes is therefore catching the cheapest
work in the search.

The only change that ever went round this wall is Section~\ref{sec:elim}: it
attacks an \emph{exponent} rather than a count, and it is worth $49\times$.
We state the wall plainly because the fifth attempt is not worth anyone's
afternoon.

\subsection{And the same wall on the proof, not only the search}
\label{sec:cert}

One more attempt deserves its own paragraph, because it tests something wider
than an optimisation.  After the walk seeds $95\%$ of the front, $93\%$ of the
remaining boxes are empty and the enumeration is almost purely a \emph{proof of
emptiness}.  But ``is there a feasible point not dominated by any of the $k$
known ones?'' is \textbf{one} question, and the box list answers it by
splitting it into hundreds.  Sylva--Crema answers it in one integer program:
$k$ cuts, built once and never iterated --- which removes the growth that made
that formulation expensive in Section~\ref{sec:cost}.

It is sound: 13 of 30 instances were proved complete by a single program, with
no wrong verdict.  It is also $4.5\times$ slower in the median and
$100\times$ slower at worst ($239$\,s against $1.95$\,s), because $k$ cuts mean
$kp$ binaries in one model --- $88$ of them at $k = 22$, $p = 4$.

That closes a question this note had left open.  The claim so far was that
criterion space is the better \emph{search}, because the model never grows.
The certificate tests whether it is also the better \emph{proof}, where there
is no iteration and no growth at all.  \textbf{It is, by $4.5\times$}:
answering $1363$ cheap questions beats answering one question with $88$
binaries.  The box decomposition is not only a better search; it is a better
proof.

\section{Three measurement lessons, and what they cost}

We report these because the direction of a result is worth less than the
knowledge of how it was checked.

\paragraph{An unsound quality metric.} Seed quality was first scored by the
ratio $\Phi(\text{seed}) / \Phi^{*}$. That ratio \emph{inverts when $\Phi^{*}$
is negative}, which it is on several instances in these families: a strictly
worse seed can score above~1. All seed-quality figures in this note use the
non-negative gap $(\Phi^{*} - \Phi(\text{seed}))/|\Phi^{*}|$, which is
well defined whatever the sign and is zero exactly when the seed is optimal.
No published number changed direction under the correction, but the metric was
wrong and one table read $1.286$ for a seed that was in fact 29\% below the
optimum.

\paragraph{Seed quality is not seed value.} On a looser instance family, the
Pareto seed is \emph{exactly optimal on 15 of 24 instances} and still buys only
$1.05\times$, because that search is not bound-limited: there are few boxes
whose bound the incumbent can defeat. Quality is a property of the heuristic;
value is a property of the pair. What is stable across all families we tried is
the \emph{ordering} --- Tchebychev loses to no seed at all ($0.73\times$,
$0.91\times$), the Pareto seed ties or wins --- not the size of any win. Single
speed-up figures quoted without their instance family should be read with this
in mind, including ours.

\paragraph{Below the noise floor, count work and not seconds.} The change of
Section~\ref{sec:ecker} removes 125 integer programs, and its wall-clock A/B
reads $0.96\times$ --- \emph{slower} --- faster on 9 of 18 instances. That
cannot be causal: removing work cannot cost time. The machine used here has a
noise floor of roughly $\pm 30\%$ per instance even at per-instance minima over
five repeats, so it cannot resolve a $6\%$ effect, and no number of repeats
fixes that. Counting integer programs and branch \& bound nodes gives the same
figure on every run and every machine, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:ecker} reports those and no seconds at all.

Two habits follow, and we adopted both only after failing at them. A
measurement taken while anything else was running on the machine is discarded,
not quoted --- the first A/B for that section was contaminated exactly so and
was re-run. And a gain predicted from the \emph{mean} cost of a call is not a
prediction: the profile that motivated the change showed the efficiency test at
$18.4$\,ms per call, the dearest operation in the search, but what the shortcut
removes is the repair's test at $7.68$\,ms, the cheap kind. Reasoning from the
wrong row of the right profile overstated the gain by half.

\section{Prior work, and what remains to be checked}

Criterion-space search for optimizing a function over the efficient set of a
multi-objective integer program is an established family, and the surveys
report it as currently more effective than decision-space search. Those methods
are for \emph{linear} objectives. The literature on a linear \emph{fractional}
objective over an integer efficient set --- including the branch-and-cut
methods this note compares against, and the extensions to fractional criteria
and to quadratic objectives --- works in decision space. The augmented weighted
Tchebychev program of Section~\ref{sec:tcheb} is standard and is used here
exactly as the references use it; nothing about it is claimed as new. The
combination treated in Section~\ref{sec:box} appears not to have been
addressed, for the reason given in Remark~\ref{rem:notZ}: $\Phi$ is a function
of $x$ and not of $\Z(x)$, so the transfer is not immediate.

\textbf{This is a statement about what we did not find, not a claim of
novelty.} A proper bibliographic review, including the sources behind
paywalls, remains to be done before any such claim is made.

\section{Reproducibility}

The implementation is pure Python with no third-party dependency, computes in
exact rational arithmetic throughout, and carries 93 tests. Several check
properties rather than answers, since a method that silently lost efficient
points would still agree with the optimum on favourable instances:

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep2pt
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:split} directly --- for every centre and every
      feasible point, the point is either removed by the cut and in no child,
      or kept and in exactly one.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:seed} operationally --- the metaheuristic is
      checked, over 14 instances and against an independent scan, never to hand
      over an inefficient point.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:tcheb} --- every optimum of \eqref{eq:tcheb} is
      checked efficient against exhaustive enumeration, and the refusals
      ($\rho \leqslant 0$, a zero weight, fractional criteria) are checked to
      fire.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:ecker} --- every witness of a failed test on
      linear criteria is checked both efficient and dominating; the shortcut is
      checked to return exactly what the walk it replaces returns, so the
      centre never moves; and on fractional criteria the walk is checked to
      still be taken and still to end efficient.
\item The early exit of Section~\ref{sec:exit} --- the run must end with boxes
      still open and unsolved while staying proved optimal and agreeing with
      the independent scan, and a seeded run, where the exit fires sooner, must
      return the same value as an unseeded one.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:front} --- the enumerated front is checked
      against exhaustive enumeration, not a sample of it; each vector's point
      is checked efficient and checked to attain that vector; the largest
      ``Phi`` on the front is checked to equal the optimum of $(P_E)$; a
      budget-limited run is checked to refuse the completeness flag; and the
      whole of it is checked again on fractional criteria, where the centre
      comes from a dominance walk rather than from
      Proposition~\ref{prop:ecker}.
\item The deliverable of Section~\ref{sec:subset} --- every member is checked
      efficient and checked to carry the value $\Phi$ actually takes there, the
      proved optimum is checked to be present and ranked first, and each source
      is checked to work alone.
\item The invariant of Section~\ref{sec:known} --- every point the search
      remembers is checked efficient, since the shortcut there cuts on one
      without re-testing it.
\item Section~\ref{sec:warm} --- a row added to a solved tableau is checked
      against rebuilding the model and solving it cold, on 120 random programs;
      and a child box's warm root is checked to reach the same relaxation value
      as its cold one.
\item The unsupported-reach claim --- on the reference instance, the three
      unsupported efficient points are all reached by \eqref{eq:tcheb} while no
      positive weighted sum reaches any.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Every comparison in this note is reproduced by scripts in the repository, run
on every commit by continuous integration, which assert that each seeded run
returns the same proved optimum as the unseeded one.
